Where it came from

PictureNet built an in-house AI cinema system for SuperStock and Stewart Cohen Productions. They needed a bespoke system that could handle full commercial production augmentation with real shot content: footage and stills from real commercial shoots, extended, restyled, matched and cut alongside generated shots, not a box that only makes clips from nothing.

That is why Gobo works the way it does. Footage you shot comes in and can be restyled, changed or made longer. Steal the Look reads the grade off a real frame, so the generated shots can match it. The script supervisor checks every cut for jumps in light, colour and grain. And every look exports as a .cube file for the editor the rest of the job lives in.

For Comfy's Open Source Higgsfield challenge (October 2026) we took that system standalone, moved it onto Comfy Router, and opened the code. Gobo is inspired by Higgsfield's Cinema Studio, which showed how good directing AI video with real camera choices can feel. Gobo is our take on that idea, built in the open.

What's inside

Make a film

The front door: one line, a look, a length and a shape. Gobo makes the film and tells you each step in plain words, then you share it with a link, or open it in the studio and change any shot.

Autopilot

The engine behind Make a film. One line in, a film out: a shot list, a keyframe for each new setup, every shot animated from it, the cuts checked, the film cut and graded.

Direction

Camera body, lens, focal length, aperture, framing, angle, light, film stock, look, grade and 28 camera moves become the prompt a DP would write.

{{ model_count }} models, one key

LTX, Wan, Seedance, Veo, Kling, MiniMax, FLUX and more through Comfy Router, each shot mapped onto each model's own inputs.

The Board

Shot cards for a spot or a short: keyframe, animate, continue one shot into the next, trim, and cut the animatic or the film.

The Lab

Steal the Look, grade like film, check continuity and draw the camera move, all in Rust, in your browser.

Open in ComfyUI

Any take, or a whole film, downloads as a ComfyUI workflow that runs on the same nodes.

How it's built

The code

A Python 3.12 server (FastAPI) and a plain-JavaScript studio page, with no front-end framework. Every paid run waits its turn in a queue on disk, and a background pump finishes it whether or not the page is still open. ffmpeg cuts, trims and encodes. The model catalogue is read from Comfy Router's published schemas, and each shot is mapped onto each model's own inputs: aspect, resolution, length, sound, first and last frames, references.

Rust, compiled to WebAssembly

The parts that have to be fast or exact are four Rust crates, named after grip gear:

direction

The shot compiler

Your direction, scene, references and palette become the prompt, in labelled pieces, so you can see which control wrote which words.

scrim

The look lab

Reads a frame, steals its look, and grades pictures. (A scrim is the mesh slid in front of a lamp to shape its light.)

flag

The script supervisor

Exposure, black level, white balance, contrast, colour, grain and framing across every cut, with a strict mode for continuous action.

dingle

Draw to direct

Strokes are simplified, measured and named: a loop is an orbit, a line toward the subject a push-in, a zigzag handheld.

One build runs in two places. In your browser, the previews and the Lab run on your own machine, and nothing is uploaded to read or grade a frame. On the server, the same .wasm files run through wasmtime to write the prompt that is actually sent and to grade your films. A test grades the same frame in both and requires identical bytes.

The grade and the LUTs

A look is a dozen numbers a colourist would recognise: exposure, white balance, contrast around a pivot, where black and white land, saturation, colour pushed into the shadows and highlights, black-and-white filters, and bleach bypass. The film stocks (Portra, Tungsten 500T, slide, expired, VHS and more) and the grades (teal and orange, Kodachrome, noir, neon and more) are presets of it, and a stock and a grade stack. The colour maths runs in linear light and Oklab.

Your picture→33 × 33 × 33 3D LUT→Halation→Vignette→Grain

The look is baked into a 33×33×33 3D LUT, and that table is exactly what the .cube export writes, so what you see in Gobo is what Resolve, Premiere or Final Cut get. What a LUT can't hold is done per pixel after it, because it needs the whole picture: halation (red light bleeding around highlights, as it does through film's anti-halation layer), a vignette, and grain that sits in the mid-tones and moves from frame to frame. An Amount control sets how much of the look reaches the picture: it blends the LUT and scales each effect. A live preview takes 10 to 16 ms a frame. To grade a take, the server decodes it to raw frames, puts each one through the same LUT and effects, and copies the sound back untouched.

Steal the Look runs the other way. It reads a frame's tone curve in Oklab, a k-means palette, the grain, the letterbox, the sharpness and the light in about 9 ms, turns the reading back into a look, and names the nearest stock and grade.

The approach

  • Measure, don't assume. The first grading pipeline darkened every film by about two levels before any look touched it. Grading a film with the look turned off showed it; the cause was ffmpeg's fast colour-space rounding, and the fix was two flags. The vignette and halation once cost 130 ms each; lookup tables took a frame from 281 ms to 15.6 ms.
  • Port without changing a word. The prompt compiler began in Python. The Rust port had to give identical output on thousands of generated shots before it ran anywhere, and only then was the Python retired.
  • Prove it live, on a budget. Every model path was tried against the real Router with the smallest run that answered the question. The whole build spent a few dollars of credit.
  • Simple on top, everything underneath. Make a film is a front door, not a second app: it drives the same studio a person would, card by card on the Board, so every film it makes can be opened and changed shot by shot.
  • Test what ships. The browser tests drive real Chrome, the WebAssembly tests run the shipped .wasm rather than a native build, and every ComfyUI export is checked against ComfyUI's own node definitions.
